# Chapter 28 ## The Steward and the Heart A steward can manage many things correctly and still have a heart that is slowly moving away from the King. He can handle money responsibly. He can lead people effectively. He can build a successful business. He can serve in ministry. He can appear generous. He can speak about faith. And yet, beneath all of these things, the heart may be seeking something other than the King. This is why stewardship is not only about what we manage. **Stewardship is also about what is happening inside us while we manage it.** The King is not merely interested in our hands. He is interested in our hearts. ### The Heart Behind the Stewardship Two people can perform the same action while having completely different motives. Two people can give the same amount. One may give out of love. Another may give because he wants recognition. Two people can serve the same ministry. One may serve because he loves God. Another may serve because he wants influence. Two people can build successful businesses. One may desire to honor God and serve people. Another may desire only wealth, status, and recognition. The outward action may look similar. But God sees what people cannot see. 1 Samuel 16:7 reminds us: > “People judge by outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart.” This truth changes the way we understand stewardship. **God does not only examine what is in our hands. He examines what is in our hearts.** ### What Owns the Heart? The heart naturally attaches itself to something. Jesus said: > “Wherever your treasure is, there the desires of your heart will also be.” — Matthew 6:21 This means our stewardship can reveal our priorities. What we consistently spend time on. What we consistently spend money on. What we protect. What we pursue. What we sacrifice for. What we become anxious about losing. These things can reveal what has captured our hearts. This is why stewardship is deeply spiritual. Our relationship with possessions is not merely financial. Our relationship with time is not merely organizational. Our relationship with influence is not merely professional. Our relationship with relationships is not merely social. Everything can reveal what our hearts value. ### When the Gift Becomes More Important Than the Giver One of the dangers of receiving God's blessings is that we can become more attached to the blessing than to the One who gave it. We may begin with gratitude: “Lord, thank You for this.” Then gradually our heart can change: “I cannot live without this.” The blessing becomes the source of security. The position becomes our identity. The money becomes our confidence. The relationship becomes our emotional foundation. The business becomes our pride. The success becomes our worth. The gift has quietly taken the place of the Giver. This is why Deuteronomy 8:18 reminds Israel: > “Remember the LORD your God. He is the one who gives you power to be successful.” The blessing should lead us toward gratitude, not away from God. ### The Danger of Loving Money Money is one of the clearest tests of the heart. Money itself is not evil. It can feed families. Build businesses. Support ministries. Help the poor. Create opportunities. Provide for legitimate needs. But Scripture warns about the love of money. Paul wrote: > “For the love of money is the root of all kinds of evil.” — 1 Timothy 6:10 Notice the issue. The problem is not simply possessing money. The problem is allowing money to possess the heart. A wealthy person can be generous and humble. A person with little can still be controlled by greed. The question is not only: **“How much money do I have?”** The deeper question is: **“How much authority does money have over my heart?”** ### When Success Becomes an Identity Success can become dangerous when it stops being something we have and becomes who we believe we are. A person may begin saying: “I am successful.” Then: “I am important because I am successful.” Eventually: “I must remain successful because without success I am nothing.” Now success has become an identity. But the steward's identity must remain anchored in the King. We are not valuable because of our position. We are not valuable because of our income. We are not valuable because of the size of our business. We are not valuable because people recognize us. Our value comes from God. This gives us freedom. We can succeed without becoming proud. We can fail without believing that our lives are worthless. We can receive promotion without worshiping the position. We can lose something without losing ourselves. ### The Heart That Wants Recognition Jesus warned about doing good things simply to be seen by others. Recognition can become addictive. We can begin serving not because we love God, but because we love being noticed. We can give and secretly wait for people to praise us. We can help someone and become disappointed when they do not thank us. We can accomplish something and become frustrated when another person receives the credit. These moments reveal something about the heart. The faithful steward learns to serve even when the applause does not come. Why? Because the King sees. Matthew 6:4 teaches that the Father sees what is done in secret. We do not need every good deed to become public. Some of our most meaningful acts of stewardship may never receive recognition. And that is okay. ### The Heart That Compares Comparison can quietly poison stewardship. We look at another person's house. Another person's business. Another person's income. Another person's influence. Another person's opportunities. Another person's success. Then we begin asking: “Why do they have more?” “Why did God give them that?” “Why am I not where they are?” Comparison can cause us to despise what the King has already entrusted to us. The faithful steward learns to appreciate his own assignment. This does not mean that we stop growing. It means we stop allowing someone else's assignment to determine our sense of worth. John 21:22 records Jesus telling Peter, in essence, to follow Him rather than become distracted by another person's path. Our responsibility is to follow the King faithfully. ### The Heart That Clings Stewardship requires open hands. Closed hands cannot easily receive. Closed hands also cannot easily release. Sometimes God asks us to release something because our relationship with it has become unhealthy. It may be money. A possession. A position. A relationship. A plan. An ambition. A dream. Even something good can become unhealthy when we refuse to surrender it to God. The question is not: **“Is this thing good?”** The question is: **“Does this thing belong in God's hands or have I placed it on the throne of my heart?”** ### Abraham and Isaac Abraham provides one of the clearest pictures of surrendered stewardship. Isaac was the son God had promised. He was precious. He was deeply loved. Yet God asked Abraham to offer Isaac. The story was a test, and God ultimately provided the sacrifice Himself. But the moment revealed something important. Abraham had to demonstrate that the promise had not become more important than the Promiser. That is a powerful stewardship principle. Sometimes God gives us something good and later asks us to surrender our attachment to it. Not necessarily because the gift is bad. But because **nothing can take the King's place.** ### Stewardship Requires Surrender A steward must be able to say: “Lord, this is Yours.” Not only when receiving. But also when releasing. If we can receive blessings but cannot release them, we may have misunderstood stewardship. The King never intended His gifts to become idols. Everything must remain surrendered. Our businesses. Our families. Our money. Our dreams. Our plans. Our future. Our influence. Our reputation. Our possessions. Our entire lives. The open hand is the posture of a steward. ### Guarding the Heart Proverbs 4:23 says: > “Guard your heart above all else, for it determines the course of your life.” Why must the steward guard the heart? Because a corrupted heart can eventually corrupt stewardship. Greed can change financial decisions. Pride can change leadership decisions. Fear can change generosity. Insecurity can change relationships. Envy can change priorities. Unforgiveness can change how we treat people. The heart directs the hands. Therefore, protecting the heart is part of protecting the stewardship. ### What Is Driving You? Every steward should periodically ask: **Why am I doing this?** Why do I want more? Why do I want this position? Why am I building this business? Why am I serving? Why am I giving? Why am I pursuing this opportunity? Why do I want recognition? Why am I afraid of losing this? These questions may expose motives we would rather not see. But honest examination is not condemnation. It is an opportunity for correction. God can purify our motives. ### From “What Can I Get?” to “What Can I Give?” The world's system often teaches us to ask: “What can I get?” The kingdom teaches us to ask: “What can I give?” How can I serve? How can I bless? How can I create value? How can I help? How can I steward this opportunity for the benefit of others? This does not mean neglecting legitimate needs. A faithful steward still plans. Still saves. Still works. Still builds. Still provides. But the heart moves from self-centered accumulation toward kingdom-minded stewardship. ### The Heart of a Servant Jesus demonstrated servant leadership. He did not use His position to elevate Himself above others. He served. He humbled Himself. He washed feet. He gave. He sacrificed. His life demonstrates that authority in God's kingdom is connected to responsibility. The greater the position, the greater the opportunity to serve. This means leadership should never be about: “How many people serve me?” It should increasingly become: “How many people can I serve faithfully?” ### The Heart Must Be Renewed We cannot transform our hearts through willpower alone. We need God's work within us. David prayed: > “Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a loyal spirit within me.” — Psalm 51:10 That should be the prayer of every steward. Not: “Lord, give me more.” But first: “Lord, make my heart right.” Because greater resources in the hands of an unhealthy heart can create greater problems. Greater influence without humility can become pride. Greater wealth without generosity can become greed. Greater authority without character can become control. Greater success without surrender can become idolatry. Therefore, before asking God for more, we should ask Him to prepare our hearts to steward more. ### The Heart That Can Say Enough Contentment is another mark of a healthy steward. Contentment does not mean refusing to grow. It means refusing to believe that more will finally make us whole. Paul wrote: > “I have learned how to be content with whatever I have.” — Philippians 4:11 The steward can pursue growth without being controlled by greed. He can build without worshiping achievement. He can prosper without becoming possessed by prosperity. He can enjoy God's blessings without making them his god. ### The Heart That Gives Thanks Gratitude protects the heart. When we recognize that everything comes from God, entitlement loses its power. Instead of saying: “I deserve this,” we begin saying: “Thank You, Lord.” Instead of: “I built everything myself,” we say: “You gave me strength, wisdom, opportunity, and resources.” Instead of: “This is mine,” we remember: “This was entrusted to me.” Gratitude keeps the steward humble. ### The Heart That Returns the Glory When something succeeds, the steward returns the glory to the King. Not because he did nothing. He may have worked hard. He may have sacrificed. He may have planned carefully. He may have made wise decisions. But he recognizes that even his ability, strength, opportunities, and breath are gifts from God. 1 Corinthians 4:7 asks: > “For what gives you the right to make such a judgment? What do you have that God hasn’t given you?” That question brings perspective. Whatever we have received is ultimately a reason for gratitude, not pride. ### The Heart of the Faithful Steward The faithful steward is not perfect. He will sometimes discover wrong motives. He will sometimes struggle with comparison. He may wrestle with greed, pride, fear, or insecurity. But he does not hide these things from God. He brings them into the light. He asks God to search him. He allows correction. He remains teachable. He continues surrendering. The goal is not to pretend that our hearts are always pure. The goal is to continually allow the King to purify them. ### A Heart Worth Trusting Imagine the King placing greater responsibility into the hands of someone whose heart is surrendered to Him. That person can receive without worshiping the gift. Lead without exalting himself. Earn without becoming greedy. Give without seeking applause. Build without becoming proud. Lose without becoming bitter. Wait without abandoning faith. That is the kind of steward God can trust with greater responsibility. The question is therefore not simply: **“Can God trust me with more?”** It is: **“Can God trust my heart with more?”** ### The Greatest Treasure Jesus said: > “Your treasure will be where your heart is.” — Matthew 6:21 The greatest treasure of the steward is not what sits in his bank account. It is not the size of his business. It is not his title. It is not his reputation. It is not what people say about him. The greatest treasure is the King Himself. When the King remains our treasure, everything else finds its proper place. Money becomes a tool. Business becomes an assignment. Influence becomes a responsibility. Possessions become resources. Relationships become opportunities to love. Time becomes an offering. Life becomes stewardship. ### A Steward's Declaration My heart belongs to the King. I will not allow what God has given me to become more important than the One who gave it. I will guard my heart against greed, pride, envy, fear, and the hunger for recognition. I will remember that everything I have is entrusted to me. I will receive with gratitude. I will manage with wisdom. I will give with generosity. I will release with surrender. I will lead with humility. I will serve with love. I will succeed without forgetting the King. I will face loss without losing my identity. I will not allow possessions to possess me. I will not allow position to define me. I will not allow recognition to control me. **The King is my treasure. The King is my source. The King is my purpose. And the King alone belongs on the throne of my heart.** ### Prayer **King of Kings,** Search my heart. Show me anything that has taken a place that belongs only to You. If I have become attached to possessions, loosen my grip. If I have become controlled by money, restore my freedom. If I have become proud of success, humble me. If I have become dependent on recognition, remind me that Your approval is greater. If I have become distracted by comparison, bring my eyes back to my own assignment. If I have allowed fear to control my decisions, teach me to trust You. Create in me a clean heart and renew a steadfast spirit within me. Teach me to receive Your blessings without worshiping them. Teach me to steward Your resources without becoming controlled by them. Teach me to lead without pride. To give without seeking applause. To work without making success my identity. To release without fear. To remain content while still growing. And above everything else, keep my heart close to You. I do not want to become a successful steward with a distant heart. I want to be a faithful steward whose heart remains surrendered to the King. **Everything I have is Yours. Everything I am is Yours. Everything I build is Yours. And my heart belongs to You.** In Jesus' name, **Amen.**
